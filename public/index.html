The assignment's questions

Answers

QUESTION 1

What assumptions did you make?

  • Amounts are USD JSON numbers. Other currencies DEFER, because the $50,000 limit can't be applied without exchange-rate evidence.
  • evaluated_at is supplied by the caller and trusted; freshness is measured against it, never the server clock.
  • account_balance carries no timestamp, so it counts as current at evaluated_at.
  • Boundaries pass: evidence exactly 30:00 old, exactly $50,000, amount equal to balance.
  • Timestamps must be ISO-8601 with a timezone. Amounts must be positive numbers, and a request_id is required.
  • Only "approved" passes the vendor rule. Any other status on fresh evidence is a HALT.
QUESTION 2

How did you distinguish DEFER from REFER?

DEFER is about missing facts. Evidence is absent, unreadable or stale, so neither ADMIT nor HALT is legitimate yet, and the same caller can fix that by resubmitting fresh evidence under a new request_id.

REFER is about missing authority. The transfer is above this agent's limit, and no amount of evidence lets this service approve it. The referral carries any open evidence questions along for the approver.

QUESTION 3

What determines rule precedence?

Authority limits who may say yes, not who may say no. Every check runs; the first gate that fires decides:

  1. Uncovered action, disallowed role or malformed request → HALT
  2. Fresh evidence shows an unapproved vendor, or amount exceeds balance → HALT
  3. Amount above the limit → REFER
  4. Needed evidence missing, unreadable or stale → DEFER
  5. Otherwise → ADMIT

So a determinable HALT beats REFER, and REFER beats DEFER because fresh evidence still couldn't yield ADMIT. Stale evidence decides nothing, which is why a stale "suspended" DEFERs. The ladder is one table, GATES, in evaluator.py.

QUESTION 4

What makes two evaluations "the same"?

  • The same policy version and policy digest.
  • The same canonical decision inputs: only the fields the rules read, with key order, number spelling (24000 vs 24000.00) and timezone spelling (Z vs +02:00) normalized. A missing field is different from null.
  • decision_id = SHA-256 of request_id, input hash and policy digest, so identical inputs always produce the identical id.
  • For retries, the ledger also compares a fingerprint of the whole canonical payload, so any change under a reused id is caught.
  • Checked by Hypothesis (400 random requests, shuffled keys) and a test that fails if decide() reads the clock.
QUESTION 5

What would have to change before you trusted it in front of an irreversible production action?

  • Server-stamped evaluation time within a bounded skew, instead of trusting the caller's.
  • Signed evidence with provenance, so balance and vendor status can't be forged by the agent.
  • Authenticated callers, with actor.id bound to the credential.
  • A durable, append-only audit log with retention, not just a retry ledger.
  • Reviewed, signed policy releases, and a staged rollout.
  • The executor redeeming each ADMIT decision_id exactly once, just before acting.
  • Alerting on HALT and DEFER rates, and an independent review of the rules.
QUESTION 6

What did you deliberately not build?

  • Authentication, authorization and rate limiting.
  • Currency conversion and evidence signatures.
  • A policy DSL or policy editor; policies are versioned Python data.
  • The referral workflow to the other authority itself.
  • Ledger retention and TTL policy.
  • Any LLM, heuristic scoring or probabilistic judgment.
